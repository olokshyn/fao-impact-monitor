# Malawi: El Niño Risk Outlook

## Past impacts

Malawi’s agricultural sector is highly exposed to El Niño-related rainfall variability because of its economic importance, the large share of livelihoods dependent on farming and limited irrigation. Agriculture, forestry and fishing accounted for about 30.0% of GDP in 2025 [1], while agriculture employed about 65.0% of the workforce [2]. Land equipped for irrigation represented only about 3.67% of cropland in 2024 [3]. El Niño is typically associated with drier conditions in Malawi, and past events show that prolonged dry spells can translate rapidly into crop losses, food-price pressure and acute food insecurity.

**Agriculture and food production**: Past El Niño events show similar trajectory of impacts on agricultural losses after a delayed onset of rains, inadequate cumulative rainfall and prolonged dry spells especially at key stages of crop development. In the 2023-24 events, maize production recorded yield loss of 17% below the five-year average yield [4]. An estimated 509,361 tonnes across maize, rice, groundnut, soya bean, sorghum and other crops were lost in 23 affected districts [5]. 93% of surveyed farmers reported lower food-crop production and 75% harvested less than half of the previous year [6].

**A recurring pattern of large production shocks linked to El Nino-related droughts**: during the 2015/16 El Niño, prolonged dry spells affected more than 30% of cultivated land – about 654,344 ha belonging to more than 1.8 million farming households. Around 270,000 ha were severely affected, and total cereal production was estimated at 40% below the previous five-year average. In 2023-24, approximately 749,113 ha of maize were affected, equivalent to 44.3% of the national maize crop area [7]. The recurrence of major crop losses in both 2015/16 and 2023/24 El Niño events shows the vulnerability of Malawi’s predominantly rainfed production to El Niño-linked rainfall deficits [8].

**Food security, household incomes and markets**: The 2023/24 production shock quickly fed through to food security, livelihoods, and markets. Close to 2 million farming households - representing more than 9 million people - were reported affected, and a state of disaster was declared in 23 of Malawi’s 28 districts [9]. More than half of respondents in an El Niño impact assessment reported losing half of their income compared with the previous year. Livestock owners reported selling animals to purchase food [10]. During December 2023-January 2024, maize-grain prices reached 230% above the five-year average, constraining household income and spending [11].

## Expected impacts

**Projected risks to agricultural production**: Current outlook shows negative impacts of El Niño-linked droughts from late 2026 as a risk to agricultural production in Malawi [12]. While the scale of losses cannot yet be quantified, the impacts from past events show a clear downward trajectory when dry conditions coincide with critical crop-development stages.

**El Niño is likely to arrive in an already constraint food security situation**: El Niño risk is emerging against an already fragile food-security and price environment. Approximately 1.9 million people were experiencing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) acute food insecurity between April and September 2026. At the Mitundu reference market, maize prices were already 40% above the five-year average in May 2026 and were projected to remain around 70% above average from June to September and about 65% above average during the October-January peak lean season [13]. A new production shock could further constrain households already facing high food costs and reduce their capacity to absorb further income or harvest losses.

**El Niño can also generate wider supply and trade pressures**: The 2023/24 drought demonstrated how domestic crop losses can spill over into national cereal supply. Following the poor 2024 harvest, Malawi’s maize import requirements for the April 2024-March 2025 marketing year were estimated to be above average. Imports were sourced from outside Southern Africa as the subregion experienced El Niño-related production declines of typical exportable surpluses. El Niño impacts were shown to have transmitted from farms to markets and the wider economy and cross borders when neighbouring countries were affected at the same time [14].

## Preparedness Considerations

Anticipatory actions for dry spells in Malawi [15] are designed to protect crop- and livestock-dependent households before erratic rainfall causes irreversible losses to harvests, livestock and nutrition. Water security measures include rehabilitation of non-functional water schemes and boreholes, paired with water-committee training and hygiene promotion. Agricultural support includes winter and off-season farming assistance, small-scale solar irrigation systems, basic irrigation packages adapted to local agro-ecological conditions, climate-smart agriculture awareness, integrated pest management, and distribution of hermetic storage bags, acaricides and drought-tolerant seed. Households receive unconditional cash assistance and Cash+ packages to protect food security and livelihoods. Nutrition and protection measures include child-malnutrition screening with therapeutic food distribution, frontline-worker training on infant and young child nutrition, GBV-prevention training and monitoring, and emergency-responder and gatekeeper training. Community groups are supported to strengthen their access to early-warning advisories and livestock vaccines. Collectively, these anticipatory actions seek to safeguard agricultural and livestock production, protect household water access, maintain child nutrition and food security, reduce negative coping strategies, and strengthen community resilience against recurring dry-spell events across the targeted districts.

Beyond short-term anticipatory actions, long-term resilience can be strengthened through the adoption of sustainable and resilient agriculture practices, restoration efforts, sustainable land use and water management, and crop diversification. Additional investments in climate-resilient crop varieties, agrometeorological services, and sustainable fishing and aquaculture systems can help rural households adapt to increasing climate variability.
