# Ethiopia: El Niño Risk Outlook

## Past impacts

Ethiopia’s agricultural sector is highly exposed to El Niño-related rainfall variability because of its economic importance, the large share of livelihoods dependent on agriculture and limited irrigation. Agriculture, forestry and fishing accounted for about 32.8% of GDP in 2025 [1], while agriculture employed about 60.1% of the workforce [2]. Irrigation remains limited, with land equipped for irrigation representing only about 4.7% of cropland in 2024 [3]. El Niño impacts are also geographically uneven. In western and northern Ethiopia, El Niño is typically associated with below-average June–September rainfall, threatening the main cropping season, while eastern Ethiopia can experience above-average October–December rainfall and flooding [4]. The consequences can therefore range from drought, crop failure and pasture shortages in one part of the country to flooding, crop and livestock losses in another.

**Agriculture and food production**: Past El Nino events show how the phenomenon built on preexisting drought vulnerabilities. In 2015-16, El Nino-related droughts followed two consecutive poor rainy seasons, beg and kiremt, leaving harvests well below average across large areas of Afar, Amhara, Oromia, Somali, Tigray and Southern Nations, Nationalities and Peoples’ Regions and losses of 50–90 percent in some others [5]. Livestock were also severely affected: drought killed hundreds of thousands of animals, while shortages of pasture and water contributed to high mortality and morbidity and abnormal livestock migration.

**Food security, nutrition and household wellbeing**: Agricultural losses rapidly translated into food insecurity and erosion of household assets. During the 2015-16 El Nino-linked drought, the estimated number of food-insecure people rose from 4.5 million in August to more than 10 million by the end of 2015 [6]. Government-led assessment identified 10.2 million food-insecure people and 2 million people requiring agricultural inputs to resume production [7]. Two failed seasons left 1.7 million households seed insecure. Pastoral households faced an additional income shock as livestock prices fell by as much as 60% in some areas [8]. Households increasingly resorted to negative coping strategies: selling livestock and other agricultural assets, exhausting seed reserves, accumulating debt and pushing them to rely on external assistance. Within 2016, the number of livestock dependent households requiring support almost tripled.

## Expected impacts

Projected losses to agricultural production: Emerging El Niño conditions in 2026 are already coinciding with rainfall deficits in key production areas. The Greater Horn of Africa outlook indicated a high likelihood of below-average June–September rainfall across most cropping areas, with likely negative impacts on yields [9]. In northern, eastern and central Ethiopia, June–July rainfall was only 40–70% of typical levels. In Amhara, nearly 20% of the planned 5.6 million hectares had yet to be planted by late July because of El Niño-driven early-season drought [10]. These conditions point to increasing risks to cereal production if rainfall deficits persist through critical crop-development stages.
Farmers and rural livelihoods are on the front line: El Niño is developing against already stressed livelihood and food-security conditions. Staple prices remain exceptionally high: in June 2026, maize prices in Sodo were more than 90% above the five-year average, while prices in Degahabur were 82% above the five-year average [11]. Millions of households are already facing Crisis (IPC Phase 3) outcomes, which are expected to persist through January 2027 in southern, southeastern and far-northern pastoral areas because of poor livestock productivity and reduced herd sizes [12]. With only about 4.9% of total credit going to agriculture, forestry and fishing in 2024 [13], many agricultural households have limited financial buffers to absorb another production shock or finance recovery.
El Niño can generate compounding risks beyond rainfall deficits: Agricultural impacts may be amplified by heat, pests, crop disease, and excess rainfall. In 2016, heavy belg rains after prolonged dry conditions submerged about 55,000 hectares of farmland, killed thousands of livestock and damaged infrastructure [14]. During the 1997–1998 El Niño, hopper bands and desert-locust swarms formed on Somalia's northwest coast and subsequently spread into Ethiopia, posing a direct threat to agricultural production and food security [15]. This means that El Niño risks to Ethiopian agriculture can persist through multiple pathways even when the immediate rainfall deficit eases.

## Preparedness Considerations

Anticipatory actions for drought in Ethiopia [16] are designed to protect pastoral and agro-pastoral livelihoods before drought conditions result in irreversible loss of crops, livestock and household income. When pre-agreed triggers are reached, farming households receive seeds of short-cycle, drought-tolerant crop and fodder varieties paired with cash, while livestock are supported through animal health treatment and supplementary feed to reduce mortality and prevent distress sales. Non-functioning water schemes are rehabilitated, and communities are supported to reach agreements on how water and pasture are shared during drought. Water and sanitation access is maintained through cleaning and de-silting of water sources, roof-water harvesting support for schools and health facilities, and pre-stocking of water-treatment materials and chemicals. A dedicated protection component runs alongside these measures during the Deyr season: distribution of dignity kits, strengthening of traditional arbitration mechanisms, and awareness-raising on child protection, with protection and GBV considerations mainstreamed across all sectors of the response. Collectively, these anticipatory actions seek to safeguard agricultural and pastoral production, preserve household water access, maintain food security, reduce negative coping strategies such as distress livestock sales, and protect the safety and dignity of the most vulnerable drought-affected households.

Anticipatory actions for floods in Ethiopia [17] are designed to protect at-risk households, farmland and infrastructure before rising water causes irreversible damage and loss of life. Early-warning messages are disseminated through SMS, radio, U-Report, local volunteer networks and local media, giving communities time to act. Unconditional cash transfers help households cover basic needs, while sandbagging protects farmland and infrastructure from inundation. Livestock are evacuated and given temporary shelter, feed, vaccination and treatment to reduce losses. A substantial water, sanitation and hygiene component includes distribution of WASH kits, water-treatment chemicals, hygiene kits and mobile water-treatment units, alongside protection and quality monitoring of water sources and provision of temporary water supply through trucking and protected boreholes. Sanitation infrastructure is strengthened through quick-deploy latrines and safe emergency sanitation sites. Public-health messaging and community engagement are carried out via radio, mobile vans and schools, complemented by targeted health and nutrition actions, including cholera-outbreak management in Gambella. Education-in-emergencies measures help maintain children's learning during displacement, and dedicated protection support is provided to internally displaced people and vulnerable host communities. Collectively, these anticipatory actions seek to safeguard lives, protect agricultural assets and water access, maintain health and hygiene standards, and reduce displacement-related risks for flood-affected communities.

Beyond short-term anticipatory actions, El Niño also presents an opportunity to invest in longer-term resilience measures. These include strengthening small-scale irrigation and dryland farming systems, promoting integrated watershed and natural resource management, and scaling up sustainable agriculture, rangeland rehabilitation and agrometeorological services. Investments in community-based natural resource governance and risk management can further enhance resilience to future droughts and climate-related shocks.
